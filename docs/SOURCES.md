## Module 8 — medication log

Read on 14 November 2026. Official NASA, NIH and FDA documents. The module **does not prescribe**: it logs what the crew member took, follows up on whether it worked and flags patterns worth raising with the crew medical officer. It never suggests a medication or a dose and never interprets a symptom.

- **NASA ISS medical kit list** — "3.001 Medical Kit – Contents and Reference" (MED CL/SpX-6, 09 APR 15), NASA public release. Gives the names the log offers, the kit categories (Pain Reliever, Sleep, Decongestant, Space Motion Sickness/Anti-Nausea, Steroid, Stool Softener — the table headings), the possible side effects the follow-up asks about, and the comments the warnings quote. Table cells are quoted word for word with line breaks removed.
- **NASA HRP evidence report "Risk Factor of Inadequate Nutrition"** (Smith, Zwart, Heer; approved for release 5 Jan 2015), https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Nutrition.pdf — vitamin D in the mission plan (pp. 54–56 and 122–123). Vitamins C, E and B were proposed by the team pharmacist and left out: the same report treats them as open questions (vitamin C p. 69, vitamin E p. 58, folate and B12 linked to vision changes p. 168), not as supplements to take.
- **NASA OCHMO technical briefs, NASA HRP and FDA labels / NIH NCCIH** — kept for the label data, the medications' performance cautions used by Fit for duty, and context. The screen no longer shows label directions or doses.

### Open points for the pharmacist

- **Acetaminophen 325 mg:** the NASA kit list says "No more than 12 tablets every 24 hours."; the current FDA label says 10. The log's warning quotes the kit list (12).
- **The kit list is from 2015;** the current ISS formulary may differ.
- **Vitamin D:** NASA's report gives 800 IU a day as the recommendation (2015). The app says "every day" and quotes the report; it does not present a dose as current.
- **Side effects** in the follow-up are the kit list's "Possible Side Effects" column, split at the commas.

### App rules in this module (illustrative, not NASA limits)

When "Did it work?" is asked after each medication · the same kit category logged on 3 days in a row → "Include it in the next downlink to Earth" · a medication that did not work twice in a row → "Talk it over with your crew medical officer" · the body zones that say what a dose was for · the alarm-sign list. Warnings that quote the kit list: more than its 24-hour limit, more days in a row than it allows, and sleep medications combined within 24 hours.

### Devices: calibration and the watch's alerts (15 Nov 2026)

**Calibration.** From the same Actiware manual (Sleep/Wake Analysis): "Sleep = Total Activity Counts ≤ Wake Threshold Value" and "Wake = Total Activity Counts > Wake Threshold Value"; for 1-minute epochs the manual's worked example weighs the minute and two on each side, "100 * (1/25) + 42 * (1/5) +20 + 13 * (1/5) + 67 * (1/25) = 37.68" (a test reproduces it); wake thresholds Low 20, Medium 40, High 80; "The higher the Wake Threshold, the fewer the number of epochs scored as wake." The app can rescore an imported 1-minute file with any of the three, or keep the score already in the file. Raising it to 80 because arms float in microgravity during sleep is **a team hypothesis, not validated**: we found no source, and the screen says so. A teammate's pyActigraphy line was not used as written: `read_raw_agd` reads ActiGraph files, and its Roenneberg `threshold` is a "Fraction of the trend" (default 0.15), not an Actiware count (https://ghammad.github.io/pyActigraphy/api.html).

**Alerts from the watch** (in the same alert bar as the live sensors, with a chime): the watch off the wrist for 60 minutes or more outside sleep and outside a spacewalk ("put it back on"), and a short night, under 7 hours of estimated sleep, against "an average adult needs 7-9 hours of sleep for optimal daytime functioning" (NASA OCHMO Sleep Accommodations, already quoted in Fit for duty); the short-night alert clears once the crew member opens Devices that day. Thresholds are app rules.

### Simulator engine from the team (illustrative app rules)

A team member's engine was merged into module 10: twelve simulations that can run together (CO₂ scrubber fault, solar particle event, cabin leak, cooling failure, fire, ammonia leak, power loss, exercise session, spacewalk, long time still, dehydration, racing heart), effort and recovery, steady alerts (raised after 3 readings in a row, cleared after 5) and priorities in an emergency. **Family channel (app idea, not a NASA system):** a family member writes a short text message that travels inside a link; on the crew member's device it arrives sealed, showing only who it is from and what kind it is; the crew member opens it, sets it aside or deletes it, and nothing is marked overdue or reported back. Messages stay on the device (never synced to other crew devices), are text only and capped at 600 characters. The demo crew has two messages marked SIMULATED. Serious or urgent news is out of scope: families go through the mission's support team.

**Family channel, delivery (app design, 14 Nov 2026; replaces "the message travels inside a link"):** the first version only produced a link that somebody had to open on the crew member's device, so nothing arrived by itself. Now each crew member has one family link (`family.html#…`) that opens a page with only the message form, already addressed to them. Tapping Send delivers it three ways, in this order: (1) straight into the inbox when the crew member uses that same browser; (2) into the crew member's mailbox on the team's sync server (`/api/sync`, the same Vercel function as station sync, unchanged), encrypted on the family's phone with a key that only travels inside the link, and collected by the crew member's app every 8 seconds while it is open; (3) where the app is served without that server (a plain web host such as GitHub Pages) the page says so and gives a link to send by hand. A new message is announced on whatever screen is open with a soft chime; it stays sealed until the crew member taps "Open it", and a paused channel announces nothing. Limits, stated plainly: anyone holding the family link can write to that mailbox and read what still waits in it, so it is for family only; the mailbox belongs to the device that created the link; the app must be open to collect and announce (a web page cannot notify with the phone locked). None of this is a NASA system.

**Family channel protections (app choices, pending review by the team's psychology lead):** a message from home can also deepen homesickness, so the crew member can pause the channel (messages are kept sealed and nothing is announced anywhere in the app); after reading, one tap records how it left them (better, about the same, more homesick), and two "more homesick" in a row suggest talking to someone or pausing; the writing page gives the family three pieces of guidance before they write. A photo (JPEG, shrunk in the browser to about 125 KB) or a voice note (up to 45 seconds) can travel with the message as a small file instead of a link; only those two formats are accepted, video is not supported, and nothing is uploaded.

**Mind map — the 3D brain (illustration and app rules):** the brain on the Mind page is drawn by the app's own code (`js/brain-core.js`: a cloud of points over a few rounded shapes, with dark wavy lines for the folds). It is not a scan, not taken from an anatomical atlas or any medical image, and nothing is measured in anyone's brain; the lobes are approximate. ## Mission Control and the analysis (15 Nov 2026)

The ground team's home is **Mission Control** (`#ground`): counts for the crew, a ranked "Look at now" list, and one card per crew member; each card opens that crew member's **analysis** (`#analysis`). The crew member's own app stays personal (their home, morning question, watch, exercise, medication log, family channel, emergency opening) and shows no readings.

**How the analysis works (predict-core.js; every threshold is an app rule, not NASA's):**
- *Their usual:* the average and spread (sample standard deviation, with a small floor per reading so a very steady fortnight does not exaggerate small changes) of the crew member's own readings over the 14 days before today, when there are at least 5.
- *Away from usual:* two spreads or more from it; flagged only in the direction that matters (sleep, oxygen, mood, exercise falling; heart rate, blood pressure, temperature rising).
- *This week:* a least-squares line through the last 7 days; called rising or falling only when it fits well (r² ≥ 0.6) and moves 1.5 spreads or more in a week, so day-to-day scatter is not read as a trend.
- *Forecast:* that line carried forward 7 days with a 95 % band (1.96 × the residual scatter, widening with distance). It shows where the readings go if nothing changes; it is not a prediction of illness.
- *Sleep debt:* hours under 7 a night over the last 7 nights (NASA cites 7–9 hours for an adult, quoted above); 4 hours or more is flagged.
- *Exercise:* the share of prescribed sessions done in 7 days; under 70 % is flagged.
- *Bone at mission's end* (app's illustrative model, not a NASA prediction): a straight line between NASA's two figures quoted above (1.5 % a month with no countermeasures; under 5 % in six months with them), by the share of exercise done, over the mission's length, marked as extrapolated past 6 months.
- *Radiation at mission's end:* the dosimeter readings logged so far, carried forward at their daily average to the mission's length, against NASA's 600 mSv career limit (quoted above).

**Demo mission (simulated):** Expedition ERG-1, a 180-day mission on a low Earth orbit station, day 14, with four fictional crew members, each telling a story the app picks up. *Valentina Reyes* (Commander): short nights, morning question unanswered, melatonin three nights, back pain after deadlifts. *Tomás Okafor* (Flight Engineer): nothing to look at, a frayed treadmill bungee reported. *Aiko Morimoto* (Mission Specialist): a six-hour spacewalk yesterday (no exercise that day), a headache, a lower mood and a request to talk. *Arjun Mehta* (Crew Medical Officer): a stuffy nose, a second sleep medication after an alarm woke him, a knee report after a run, and an opening of his own data during a loss of signal. No real astronaut is used: inventing health readings under a real person's name would be fabricating records about them.

**Medication log, one per crew member:** each dose now carries the crew member it belongs to, so on a shared device each profile (and the ground team looking at it) sees only that crew member's doses; doses saved before profiles existed stay visible to everyone because they cannot be attributed.


## Data access, emergency code, drill, clocks and export (15 Nov 2026)

App rules, not NASA procedures:

- A crew member sees none of their readings unless the ground medical team shares them, item by item, or approves a request. An approval gives a one-time 6-digit code that the ground reads to the crew member; typing it opens those items for 1 hour, 24 hours or 7 days, as the ground chose. Five wrong codes in a row stop the box until the screen is opened again.
- The crew's emergency code (6 digits, set by the ground for the mission and given to the whole crew) opens a crew member's data for 60 minutes at once, without waiting for the ground, in an emergency such as CPR on a crewmate. An alarm no longer opens the data by itself: it asks for the code. Every opening is kept and shown to the ground. The demo mission's code is shown on the profiles screen.
- The cardiac arrest drill is a simulation: no reading is written to anyone's log, and the opening it records carries a drill flag. It gives no medical instructions; the page says the crew follows the station's emergency procedures and the flight surgeon on the ground leads.
- Clocks: records are kept in UTC; Houston, Texas and Cape Canaveral, Florida are shown beside it, converted by the browser's time-zone database (America/Chicago and America/New_York, daylight saving included). Which clocks to show is an app choice; the app does not claim which time zone the station's crew keeps.
- Excel export: what the app already keeps, between two UTC dates (both included), for the crew members chosen. Simulated values keep their SIMULATED mark.

## Mental health check-ins: validated questionnaires (15 Nov 2026)

Screening, not diagnosis. Wording checked word for word on the pages below (University of Washington National HIV Curriculum pages for the PHQ and GAD; Carnegie Mellon University for the PSS-4; the authors manuscript on PubMed Central for the 3-item UCLA scale).

**PHQ-2 / PHQ-9** (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-2, https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-9). Lead-in: "Over the last 2 weeks, how often have you been bothered by the following problems?" Answers: Not at all = 0, Several days = 1, More than half the days = 2, Nearly every day = 3.

1. Little interest or pleasure in doing things
2. Feeling down, depressed or hopeless
3. Trouble falling asleep, staying asleep, or sleeping too much
4. Feeling tired or having little energy
5. Poor appetite or overeating
6. Feeling bad about yourself - or that you're a failure or have let yourself or your family down
7. Trouble concentrating on things, such as reading the newspaper or watching television
8. Moving or speaking so slowly that other people could have noticed. Or, the opposite - being so fidgety or restless that you have been moving around a lot more than usual
9. Thoughts that you would be better off dead or of hurting yourself in some way

- "If the score is 3 or greater, major depressive disorder is likely." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-2)
- "Patients who screen positive should be further evaluated with the PHQ-9, other diagnostic instruments, or direct interview to determine whether they meet criteria for a depressive disorder." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-2)
- "The PHQ-9 is a multipurpose instrument for screening, diagnosing, monitoring and measuring the severity of depression." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-9)
- "Question 9 is a single screening question on suicide risk." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/phq-9)
- Bands on the PHQ-9 page: 0-4 None-minimal, 5-9 Mild, 10-14 Moderate, 15-19 Moderately Severe, 20-27 Severe.

**GAD-2 / GAD-7** (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/gad-2, https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/gad-7). Same lead-in and answers.

1. Feeling nervous, anxious or on edge
2. Not being able to stop or control worrying
3. Worrying too much about different things
4. Trouble relaxing
5. Being so restless that it is hard to sit still
6. Becoming easily annoyed or irritable
7. Feeling afraid as if something awful might happen

- "Using a cut-off of 3 the GAD-2 has a sensitivity of 86% and specificity of 83% for diagnosis generalized anxiety disorder." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/gad-2)
- "Using a cut-off of 8 the GAD-7 has a sensitivity of 92% and specificity of 76% for diagnosis generalized anxiety disorder." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/gad-7)
- "No permission required to reproduce, translate, display or distribute." (https://www.hiv.uw.edu/page/mental-health-screening/gad-7)
- Bands on the GAD-7 page: 0-4 Minimal, 5-9 Mild, 10-14 Moderate, greater than 15 Severe (the page leaves 15 unlabelled; the app counts 15 as severe). The page recommends 8 or more as a reasonable cut-point for probable generalized anxiety disorder.

**PSS-4** (https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/.doc/pss_4_item.doc; scoring https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/html/pssscoring.html; scales page https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/index.html). Lead: "The questions in this scale ask you about your feelings and thoughts during the last month." Answers: Never = 0, Almost Never = 1, Sometimes = 2, Fairly Often = 3, Very Often = 4.

1. In the last month, how often have you felt that you were unable to control the important things in your life?
2. In the last month, how often have you felt confident about your ability to handle your personal problems?
3. In the last month, how often have you felt that things were going your way?
4. In the last month, how often have you felt difficulties were piling up so high that you could not overcome them?

- "PSS-4 scores are obtained by reverse coding items # 2 and 3." (https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/html/pssscoring.html)
- "The Perceived Stress Scale is not a diagnostic instrument; there are no score cut-offs." (https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/index.html)
- "Submitting a request for permission to use the PSS is completely free of charge and will ensure an optimized service." (https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/psychology/stress-immunity-disease-lab/scales/index.html) **To do before real use:** the team submits the free permission request for the PSS on ePROVIDE (MAPI Research Trust), as the page asks.

**3-item UCLA Loneliness Scale** (Hughes, Waite, Hawkley & Cacioppo 2004, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394670/). Lead: "The next questions are about how you feel about different aspects of your life." The paper asks by telephone, with the answers inside the question (for example "How often do you feel left out: Hardly ever, some of the time, or often?"); the app shows the question and the three answers as buttons: Hardly ever = 1, Some of the time = 2, Often = 3.

1. How often do you feel that you lack companionship?
2. How often do you feel left out?
3. How often do you feel isolated from others?

- "As in the R-UCLA, each person's responses to the questions are summed, with higher scores indicating greater loneliness." (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2394670/) The paper gives no cut-off for "lonely"; the app does not use one.

App rules (not from the sources): PHQ-2 + GAD-2 every 14 days (the questionnaires ask about the last 2 weeks); PSS-4 + UCLA-3 every 28 days (the PSS asks about the last month); the rest of the PHQ-9 / GAD-7 only after a positive PHQ-2 / GAD-2 (3 or more, the sources cut-off); the ground can ask for one at any time. Flags for the ground: PHQ-9 question 9 above "Not at all" = Act now and a request to talk is sent; PHQ-9 15 or more or GAD-7 15 or more = Act now; PHQ-9 10 or more, GAD-7 8 or more, or a positive PHQ-2 / GAD-2 = Take a look; PSS-4 up 4 or more or UCLA-3 up 2 or more since their last answer = Take a look. The crew member sees no score. Questions are shown in English: a validated Spanish version was not verified (phqscreeners.com hides its translations behind a terms page; the CMU list has no 4-item Spanish PSS).
